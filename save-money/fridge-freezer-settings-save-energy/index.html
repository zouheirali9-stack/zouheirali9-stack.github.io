Best Fridge and Freezer Settings to Save Energy

Set your fridge to 40°F (4°C) or below and your freezer to 0°F (-18°C), then use a few easy habits to cut wasted energy without putting food safety at risk.

Under $10 for an appliance thermometer

What you’ll need

  • A glass of water
  • A sheet of paper
  • Soft brush or vacuum with a brush attachment

Quick answer: keep the refrigerator at or below 40°F (4°C) and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C), the targets recommended by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Check them with an inexpensive appliance thermometer instead of trusting the dial. Running colder than that wastes energy without making food meaningfully safer, while running warmer puts food at risk.

The settings are only half the story. How you load, place and maintain the fridge affects its energy use just as much, and most of those habits cost nothing.

The two numbers that matter

The FDA’s guidance is simple: 40°F (4°C) or below in the fridge, 0°F (-18°C) in the freezer. Food kept frozen at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, although its quality slowly declines over time.

Many fridges don’t display a temperature at all. Their dial runs from 1 to 5 or 1 to 9, and the numbers mean different things on different models. That is why a thermometer is worth the small cost: it tells you what the inside actually is, rather than what the dial suggests.

How to check your real temperatures

  1. Put an appliance thermometer in a glass of water on a middle shelf of the fridge. A glass of water reads more steadily than air, which warms up every time the door opens.
  2. Put a second thermometer between frozen packages in the freezer.
  3. Leave both overnight without adjusting anything, then read them first thing in the morning, before the doors have been opened much.
  4. If the fridge reads above 40°F, turn the control one step colder. If it reads well below, around 34°F or less, try one step warmer.
  5. Wait another 24 hours and check again. Fridges take time to settle, so make one change at a time.

Mind the coldest spot

Fridges are rarely the same temperature everywhere. The back of the bottom shelf is often coldest and the door is usually the warmest part, so keep milk and raw meat on the shelves and use the door for condiments and drinks.

Habits that cut wasted energy

These changes don’t touch the temperature at all, and they help the appliance run less often.

  • Give it room to breathe. The coils that release heat need airflow. Leave space between the fridge, the wall and the cabinets, and keep it away from the oven, the dishwasher and direct sunlight, as Virginia Cooperative Extension’s energy guidance recommends.
  • Clean the condenser coils. Dust and pet hair on the coils, found at the back or behind a grille at the bottom, make the compressor work harder. Brush or vacuum them once or twice a year, more often if you have pets.
  • Test the door seals. Close the door on a sheet of paper. If it slides out with no resistance, the seal may not be closing well at that point. Clean the gasket with warm soapy water and try again; if it is torn or flattened, a replacement gasket for your model is usually available from the manufacturer.
  • Let leftovers cool briefly, then refrigerate. Divide large batches into shallow containers so they chill quickly. Don’t leave cooked food out for more than two hours.
  • Don’t leave the door open while you decide what to eat. Plan, open, take, close.
  • Defrost manual-defrost freezers before the ice builds up thickly. Frost acts as insulation on the wrong side and makes the freezer work harder.

Unplug before cleaning the coils

Pull the fridge out gently, unplug it before you brush or vacuum the coils, and plug it straight back in when you’re finished. Don’t use sharp tools near the coils or tubing; a bent or punctured line can’t be fixed at home.

Full, but not packed

A reasonably full fridge holds its temperature better after the door is opened, because the cold food acts as a buffer. Packing it so tightly that air can’t circulate has the opposite effect, leaving warm pockets near the front. Aim for shelves that are well used but still have gaps between items, and keep the vents inside clear.

The same idea applies to freezers. A full freezer recovers quickly. If yours is half empty, frozen bottles of water fill the space and double as ice packs for a cooler bag.

When an old fridge is the real problem

If your fridge is old, settings and habits can only do so much. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that how you use a refrigerator matters as much as its built-in efficiency, but its kitchen appliance fact sheet also suggests replacing units more than about 15 years old. When you shop, compare the yellow EnergyGuide label, which shows an estimated yearly energy use, and remember that a second fridge running in the garage adds to your bill all year round. If you no longer need it, unplug it and recycle it properly.
